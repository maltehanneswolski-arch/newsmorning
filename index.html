Background on today's names

Person 🇪🇺 🇩🇪

Ursula von der Leyen

b. 1958 · Germany

President of the European Commission

Physician by training, German defence minister 2013-2019, then the first woman to lead the Commission, now in a second term. Her working method is to concentrate files in her own private office rather than distribute them across portfolios.

Why it matters. The question of who speaks for Europe in the Ukraine talks runs straight through her office, where her chief of staff has accumulated the file, and the 70 billion euro ask of six non-EU capitals is hers to land. Both are jobs the treaties do not clearly give her, which is why the argument is happening in public.

She has lived in Brussels twice: as a child in the 1960s when her father worked for the Commission, and since 2019 in an apartment inside the Berlaymont itself.

Institution 🇪🇺

European Commission

Brussels · 27 commissioners

Sole right of legislative initiative · negotiates trade agreements for the Union

Proposes the budget and every piece of EU law, and negotiates trade on behalf of all member states. It has no competence over the common foreign and security policy, which belongs to the Council and the member governments acting by unanimity.

Why it matters. That split is the whole reason Europe cannot answer who would sit opposite Putin. The Commission can assemble money, tariffs and accession leverage at speed; it cannot put a negotiator in a room on the Union's behalf, and no amount of institutional weight in Brussels changes that without the capitals agreeing first.

Its monopoly on legislative initiative means the Parliament and the Council can ask for a law but cannot write one, a design borrowed from the 1951 Coal and Steel Community High Authority.

Putin takes the ninth Witkoff meeting and floats a summit that leaves Kyiv and Brussels out

Vladimir Putin agreed on Wednesday to resume US-mediated talks with Ukraine as soon as next month, the Kremlin said, after a ninth in-person meeting with Steve Witkoff in a St Petersburg imperial palace that opened with Russia releasing Stephen Hubbard, a retired American schoolteacher held since 2022. Witkoff's own proposal runs further than a trilateral: a US-China-Russia summit next month, a format that seats Beijing and leaves both Kyiv and the EU outside the room. Brussels has no agreed answer to who would speak for it, and the argument is now being had in the open, with Ursula von der Leyen's chief of staff Björn Seibert having accumulated the file and Finland's Alexander Stubb floated as a direct channel to Putin. Czech President Petr Pavel put the slippage down to Washington's diesel deal with Moscow, which he says handed the Kremlin new hope and cost the negotiation its momentum. Three capitals setting terms and a fourth being asked to underwrite them is the shape the next six weeks could take, and the EU has roughly that long to decide whether it wants a negotiator or a cheque book.

Undersea deterrence has no settled law and no reliable attribution

Telecommunications cables, power lines and pipelines are carrying more load than ever with almost no protection, and the attacks on them combine low-tech, precise and hard-to-detect methods. A severed cable is noticed at once; a damaged one can fail days, weeks or months later, which breaks the link between act and consequence that deterrence depends on. The legal authorities governing undersea action are poorly defined, so advantage goes to states that can establish what happened quickly and demonstrate a response without escalating. The parallel finding on digital networks is that China's edge comes from bundling hardware, software, financing, training and diplomacy into one offer that US firms cannot match piece by piece.
