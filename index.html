Background on today's names

Person 🇮🇹

Giorgia Meloni

Italy · prime minister since October 2022

President of the Council of Ministers of Italy · leader of Fratelli d'Italia · former president of the European Conservatives and Reformists Party

Took office in October 2022 at the head of a right-wing coalition and has since held it together longer than most Italian governments manage, pairing fiscal orthodoxy in Brussels with a harder line at home on migration and public order. The combination is what lets her answer a cost-of-living protest with troops and tougher sentencing while still defending the budget that provoked it.

Why it matters. Soldiers deployed in Milan this weekend and new penalties aimed at minors make her response the template other EU governments will study as the protests spread. Italy is also the second-largest exposure in the European luxury selloff, so the same week tests her on both the street and the balance sheet.

Her party traces its lineage to the Movimento Sociale Italiano, founded in 1946, which makes the Fratelli d'Italia flame one of the oldest continuously used symbols in Italian politics.

Institution 🇮🇹

Ministero dell'Interno

Italy · ministry of the interior

Public order and civil protection · commands the Polizia di Stato · authorises military support to policing under Strade Sicure

The ministry carries Italian public order, runs the state police and signs off the army deployments that put soldiers on city streets under the Strade Sicure framework, a standing arrangement that has existed since 2008 and is normally used for fixed-site guarding rather than for crowd events.

Why it matters. Using a counter-terrorism-era deployment mechanism against cost-of-living demonstrations is the decision worth watching, because it sets how far an EU member state can go before the question becomes a rule-of-law one rather than a policing one.

Italy has five national police forces with overlapping remits, and this ministry commands only one of them.

Starlink comes back to speed and nobody will say who slowed it

Ukraine's defence ministry said on Saturday that Starlink and the military-grade Starshield were running at pre-restriction speeds again after more than a week of degradation that had slowed drone communications during Russian missile strikes. Elon Musk's first public comment came hours later and accused the Ukrainian military of breaching SpaceX's terms of service. Both the Biden and Trump administrations have asked SpaceX over the past several years to switch the service on or off in various countries, which makes this episode an established procedure rather than one proprietor's improvisation. Taipei read it within a day: analysts there are arguing against relying on a single foreign commercial provider for wartime communications, the exact exposure Kyiv has just demonstrated at scale. No equivalent constellation is available to buy today, so for European capitals the replacement question is a procurement timetable measured in years, not a decision that can be taken this winter.

Brexit turns ten with the next EU-UK summit three weeks away

Ten years on from the referendum of 23 June 2016, the structural reading is that Britain's uncodified constitution let a single-round plebiscite settle a membership question that an entrenched system would have routed through amendment procedures and supermajorities. The United Kingdom has cycled through a long list of prime ministers since, ending for now with Andy Burnham, and the record of that decade is being judged separately from the decision that opened it. Nigel Farage spent years as a member of the European Parliament being written off as a clown by colleagues who were drawing a salary from the institution he campaigned against, which was a sustained misreading of where the pressure was accumulating. The next EU-UK summit is set for Brussels on 20 November, five days before the Western Balkans summit. The procedural lesson for the EU side is the one worth carrying into any future membership referendum: a reversible majority can produce an irreversible answer when nothing in the constitutional order requires it to be confirmed twice.
