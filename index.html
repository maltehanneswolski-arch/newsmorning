Know the players

Background on today's names

Person

Christine Lagarde

France · b. 1956

President of the European Central Bank 2019-27 · IMF Managing Director 2011-19 · French Finance Minister 2007-11

A competition lawyer by training rather than an economist, and the first woman to run the French finance ministry, the IMF and the ECB. Her presidency covered the pandemic emergency purchases, the 2022-23 tightening cycle that took the deposit rate from minus 0.5 to 4 percent, and the easing that followed.

Why it matters. She announced on 29 October that she will leave on 7 February 2027, nine months early, which hands the succession to a European Council that still has Emmanuel Macron in it and opens three senior ECB posts at once.

She captained the French national synchronised swimming team as a teenager and has said the sport taught her to smile while holding her breath.

Institution

European Central Bank

Europäische Zentralbank · Frankfurt am Main

Monetary authority for the 20 euro-area member states · banking supervisor for the largest euro-area banks

Sets the single monetary policy for the euro area and, since 2014, directly supervises around 110 significant banks through the Single Supervisory Mechanism. Its president is appointed by the European Council by qualified majority for a single, non-renewable eight-year term.

Why it matters. Lagarde's early exit opens the presidency, the markets seat Isabel Schnabel vacates for the IMF and the chief economist's post in the same window, so the next few months reset the Executive Board rather than replace one name on it.

Its Frankfurt headquarters incorporates the Grossmarkthalle, the city's 1928 wholesale market hall, from which Jews were deported between 1941 and 1945; a memorial on the site is part of the building.

Three seats open at once in Frankfurt

Christine Lagarde leaves the ECB presidency on 7 February, nine months before her term runs out, and the timing is the substance: the appointment falls to a European Council that still contains Emmanuel Macron, ahead of a French presidential election that could change who speaks for Paris. Two further senior posts turn over in the same window, with Isabel Schnabel going to the IMF and vacating the markets portfolio and the chief economist's seat also in play, so the Executive Board is being rebuilt rather than patched. Klaas Knot, formerly of De Nederlandsche Bank and a hawk who has moved toward the centre, and Pablo Hernández de Cos, now running the Bank for International Settlements and closer to the dovish end, are the names circulating for the presidency. The post is a single non-renewable eight-year term decided by qualified majority, which means capitals will trade the three appointments against each other rather than settle them one at a time. Lagarde has said publicly that better candidates than her are available; the harder question for member states is whether the successor inherits the current easing stance or a board with three new voices in it.

The AI build-out gets repriced, and Seoul banks the difference

Alphabet lost close to 500 billion dollars of market value in a single session, falling almost 13 percent, after quarterly results showed data-centre costs running well ahead of what AI search earns; it is one of the largest one-day losses on record. Amazon disclosed that cash outflow had risen nearly sixfold over three months to 42.4 billion dollars and paired it with a weak Christmas outlook, and Intel fell close to 20 percent after hours, blaming AI-driven memory prices for collapsing PC demand. The Nasdaq closed 1.4 percent down, the steepest of the three main US indices. The same memory squeeze is the other half of a Korean windfall: Samsung Electronics booked the biggest quarterly operating profit in its history on 29 October, SK hynix passed 100 trillion won of quarterly revenue for the first time, and Samsung is raising capital spending after selling out its AI chips. Chinese equities moved the other way, shedding trillions as the domestic AI race repriced, which leaves Europe exposed at the layer absorbing the cost and thin at the layer collecting the margin.

Montreal drops the word and keeps the file

The 21st EU-Canada summit opened in Montreal on 29 October with von der Leyen's offer of associate membership replaced in the drafting by ever closer association, after German legal objections to a status the treaties do not provide for. The phrase arrived in her State of the Union speech a month ago, surprised diplomats who had not been consulted, and was never specified in rights or obligations. What survives is the working agenda: critical raw materials, defence industrial cooperation, and the unratified parts of CETA, where dairy market access remains the obstacle that keeps several national parliaments from moving. Both sides are explicit about why now, with Washington absorbed by the midterms and European politics liable to shift again within a year. Costa used the Business Forum and the summit dinner to put the relationship in those terms, which leaves the result to be judged on the communiqué rather than the label.

More defence money, the same fragmented market

EU member states spent 417.62 billion euros on defence in 2025 against 239.75 billion in 2022, a 74 percent nominal rise and 56 percent once inflation is stripped out, on EUISS calculations from European Defence Agency and NATO data. Equipment spending alone is set to more than double between 2022 and 2026, yet only 24 percent of EU defence equipment procurement is collaborative against a 35 percent benchmark, so the additional money is buying national variants in small lots. The Bruegel proposal is to give the Multilateral Defence Mechanism pooled multi-year orders with contracts open to suppliers from every participating state, beginning with 155mm ammunition, and eventually to merge it with the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank so that procurement and industrial finance sit under one roof. Industry describes the same problem from the supply side: Arsenal JSCo in Bulgaria reports capacity it cannot fill because demand arrives in fragments, and Fincantieri's chief executive wants common naval requirements to cut unit costs and improve interoperability. National budgets remain the main pillar underneath all of it, with European Investment Bank lending, private capital and a possible second round of the Security Action for Europe instrument layered on top, and none of them removes the fiscal choice.

Kim Jong Un attended a drone exhibition on Thursday at which the North ran live-fire demonstrations of loitering munitions, cruise missiles and a 600mm multiple rocket launcher, and told its scientists to accelerate work on military artificial intelligence and unmanned aircraft. POLITICO Europe: 11.2 billion dollars and a single issue

Political advertising across the 2025 and 2026 calendar years has passed 11.2 billion dollars on AdImpact's count, overtaking the 2024 presidential cycle to become the most expensive election in American history. Within that, 801 million dollars went on affordability advertising in the top races alone, 401 million from Democrats and 311 million from Republicans defending the 2025 tax cuts. The gap with Europe is regulatory rather than cultural: all British parties together spent 94.5 million pounds in the 2024 general election, and the 2022 French presidential campaign cost 83.5 million euros. Petrol now averages 4.22 dollars a gallon, and the Reuters/Ipsos tracker has the Democratic advantage widening from two points in late July, when prices were at their lowest since the Iran war began, to 13 points three months later. Republican money is concentrated in Senate holds such as Texas, so a better-than-expected result on 3 November would read in Brussels as spending beating the cost-of-living argument, with consequences for trade and defence files that run through Congress.

Discipline is what the plenum produced

General Zhang Shengmin, who ran the PLA's anti-corruption apparatus from 2018 until July and was made a vice-chair of the Central Military Commission last year, has been promoted to the Politburo, placing the enforcer of the military purge inside the party's top decision-making body. The plenum closed with a record number of expulsions and with party discipline as its organising language, which observers read as consolidating authority rather than completing a cleanup. Inside the armed forces the companion policy is thrift: second-hand briefcases, salvaged screws and steel bars, reused decorations, formalised in the 15th five-year plan for 2026 to 2030 as building the forces through diligence and frugality and pursuing high efficiency at low cost. Purge above and austerity below point the same way, toward a budget expected to buy capability rather than overhead across the plan period. The number worth tracking from Brussels is whether procurement volumes hold while the leadership layer keeps turning over.

Lula's margin and the arithmetic that follows it

Lula took the 25 October run-off with 51.8 percent of valid votes to Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.2, 64.26 million votes and a margin of 4.43 million, and the loser has since claimed electoral fraud without evidence from Miami, having left Brazil via Argentina and Ecuador. For Brussels the practical consequence is a counterpart that wants Mercosur ratification and climate cooperation handled multilaterally, and von der Leyen read the result as a win for democracy and multilateralism. Beijing reads the same count as preserving its main foothold in Latin America and as leverage over Brazilian rare earths, which touches the EU's own critical raw materials diversification directly. The defeat complicates Washington's effort to assemble an aligned right-wing bloc across the Americas and opens a contest over who now leads the Brazilian right. The constraint on all of it is the domestic economy, which will set how much foreign policy this term can actually finance.
