Background on today's names

Person

Steve Witkoff

United States · special envoy since 2025

US special envoy · lead American negotiator on Ukraine and on the Iran file

A New York property developer with no diplomatic career before 2025, he was given the envoy portfolio and has since run Washington's direct channels to Moscow, Kyiv and Tehran in parallel, which is why the same name sits above both the Ukraine track and the Gulf one.

Why it matters. He announced that American, Russian and Ukrainian officials will meet in Abu Dhabi next week, the first trilateral since February, which puts the shape of any ceasefire in the hands of a channel Europeans do not sit in.

The envoy brief he holds carries no Senate confirmation, so the negotiator Europe deals with on Ukraine answers to the president alone.

Institution

US Congress

United States Congress · Capitol Hill, Washington

Legislature of the United States · 435-seat House elected every two years · 100-seat Senate, a third of it each cycle

Holds the power of the purse and ratifies treaties by a two-thirds Senate vote, but has delegated most tariff and sanctions authority to the executive through standing statutes a president can invoke without a fresh vote.

Why it matters. Today's midterms decide control of both chambers for the rest of this presidential term, and with it the appropriations that fund Ukraine aid and the committee seats that scrutinise tariff and sanctions decisions.

The last tariff schedule Congress wrote itself was the Smoot-Hawley Act of 1930; every rate since has moved under authority it delegated away.

Europe prices in a weakened Trump rather than a friendlier Washington

Polls open across the United States today for all 435 House seats and 35 Senate races, deciding who controls Congress for the rest of this presidential term. Brussels is not reading a Democratic night as relief: the legislature has few instruments to constrain a president on foreign policy, so tariff authority, sanctions designations and the Ukraine file stay with the executive whoever wins the chambers [POLITICO Europe, EURACTIV]. Trump finished near a career-low approval rating and Georgia set an early-voting record [Al Jazeera, BBC News Briefing]. The operative European assumption is that a president facing a hostile Congress reaches for the instruments that need no vote, which is what made the past 48 hours busy: strikes on Iran, and an Abu Dhabi date on Ukraine.

A date in Abu Dhabi, nine three-tonne bombs in Kherson

American, Russian and Ukrainian officials will meet in Abu Dhabi next week, the first trilateral since February, and the two sides are not bringing the same agenda: Moscow wants European security architecture reopened, meaning NATO back behind its 1997 line, while the Europeans want a ceasefire and security guarantees first [EURACTIV, POLITICO Berlin Playbook]. The six-day Kherson truce, the longest of the war, expired without violations and Russia resumed at once: at least nine 3,000kg aerial bombs hit a school distributing aid, killing 26 [BBC News - Europe]. Berlin is buying standing rather than waiting for it, with a 200 million euro winter package split between the foreign and development ministries and a coalition-of-the-willing summit at the Chancellery in a fortnight.

Freezers in eight countries, false nuclear alerts in two

Refrigeration systems in supermarkets across at least eight European countries were driven to 50C overnight on Monday, forcing stock to be destroyed in Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden, three weeks after the same pattern hit hundreds of French grocers. Agriculture Commissioner Christophe Hansen says food security is not at risk but has opened a Commission inquiry into supply-chain resilience, and US investigators are checking whether the French wave links to refrigeration failures at American bases in August [POLITICO Europe, New York Times - Europe]. A separate pair of intrusions pushed false air-raid and nuclear warnings through Ukrainian and Russian alert apps; Zelensky confirmed Kyiv struck back after Moscow went first [PBS NewsHour].

Beijing's strategists worry about being dragged in by other people's wars

Chinese establishment commentary in October moved its anxiety off Washington and onto Washington's allies. Da Wei calls the relationship an L-shaped stabilisation rather than an improvement and expects more trouble after the midterms; Zheng Yongnian concedes that Beijing's preferred multipolarity is only an ideal state and describes power beyond the two giants as feudalising. The sharper argument is about entrapment: Qi Haixia and Yao Yunhe read Takaichi's Japan as capable of pulling the United States into a Taiwan confrontation, a dynamic Yao calls reverse entrapment, while Jia Min warns against great powers being held hostage by small countries. A CASS scholar, Pang Dapeng, casts Russia itself as a structural constraint on US-China stability [Sinocism].

Washington strikes Qeshm while Tehran offers to reopen Hormuz in two days

US forces hit radar and surface-to-air missile sites on Iran's Qeshm Island hours before polls opened, framed by officials as defensive protection for commercial shipping; the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps says it downed an MQ-9 over the strait [Financial Times, bne IntelliNews]. Tehran has cut its offer to reopen the blockaded Strait of Hormuz from seven days to two if Washington meets its conditions, and Khamenei has widened the president's negotiating authority, while Netanyahu presses to finish the job first [France 24]. Brent fell 6.5 percent to 105.55 dollars, a two-week low. A Qatari delegation is in Tehran, and Wang Yi told Iranian, Saudi and Omani counterparts that negotiation is the only way out of a conflict already spilling over.

Briefing🇵🇱 🇪🇺 🇷🇺 2h ago

Poland's court fight hardens into the 2027 campaign

Tusk's removal of Law and Justice appointees from the Constitutional Tribunal is now the organising line of Polish politics ahead of the 2027 election, with the government calling it restoration of the rule of law and the opposition calling it a coup [POLITICO Europe]. President Karol Nawrocki's refusal to swear in judges elected by the Sejm is the blockage the intervention is meant to break, and Tusk's camp is betting that Brussels tolerates the method [EURACTIV, EUobserver]. Moscow has noticed: after losing sympathetic governments in Hungary and Serbia, Russian messaging is working the standoff to weaken Polish support for Ukraine [New York Times - Europe]. The cost of being right by the wrong route is a precedent the next majority inherits.
