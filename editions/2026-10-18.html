A sixty-day pause on Russia sanctions, and a gas offer Europe did not ask for

Trump signed an executive order late on Friday waiving key provisions of the Graham Act, the law authorising punitive tariffs on buyers of Russian oil, days before the measures were due to bite and after cutting a deal with Moscow to release diesel. Rubio put the suspension at 60 days and framed it as time to study what sanctions and tariffs would do to the global and American economies, with energy prices high and the Iran war running. At least eight Republican senators and several House members have broken with the president, Rick Scott restating that Russia is the aggressor and Thom Tillis calling the order beyond irresponsible, yet nobody in Congress has a fast route to reverse an executive waiver of a law it passed overwhelmingly. The administration is separately pitching expanded Russian gas sales into Europe as a component of a Ukraine settlement, which puts the burden of refusal on capitals that have spent four years paying to build replacement supply. Zelensky expects further relief to follow and has asked Europe to open its own channel to Moscow, and that request, not the waiver itself, is what lands on the EEAS and the next Foreign Affairs Council.

Kyiv satellite link throttled, and nobody in Washington will own the order

Speed restrictions on Starshield terminals in Ukraine were a planned operation rather than a fault, Zelensky said, after a test showed no equivalent limits on the service in other European countries. The denial from Washington is that nobody at the White House ordered the slowdown, which was set to stop the terminals working on drones travelling faster than 100mph. Kyiv has abandoned the short truce on energy targets it had been observing and struck a Russian refinery, saying Moscow never honoured it, while Russian air defences claimed 195 drones destroyed over Moscow overnight and Domodedovo and Vnukovo suspended flights. Zelensky put it as losing a strong partner, and in the same breath asked Europe to negotiate with Moscow on its own account, while Rustem Umerov met Jared Kushner on Friday to work on a three-way meeting with Trump and Putin. The operative European question is not who gave the order but who replaces the capacity, on what contract and before which winter strike campaign.

Six attacks in ten days put merchant shipping inside NATO exclusive economic zones

Suspected Russian air and naval drones have hit commercial vessels six times in ten days inside Bulgarian and Romanian waters, killing two people and leaving at least 25 missing on a route that carries a large share of Black Sea trade. The Turkish-owned Alfa Watan, bound for the Romanian port of Sulina, was struck in Bulgaria's exclusive economic zone on 6 October, and the ferry that reached the scene found an overturned lifeboat and lifejackets but no trace of the ten crew. Romanian F-16s shot down two drones roughly 20 kilometres southeast of Sulina on Saturday, and Sofia has asked NATO for anti-drone systems after two more ships caught fire off the Romanian coast this week. Risk that sits inside allied maritime jurisdiction rather than in a declared war zone is a different legal problem from a strike on Odesa, and it reprices insurance, crewing and routing for the whole basin. The question now is whether cover for merchant traffic becomes an allied task with a standing patrol, or stays two national air forces reacting to each incident.

Mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and the first ship Iran says has sunk

Two tankers carrying about two million barrels of crude each caught fire after striking mines in the Strait of Hormuz, which the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps attributes to their use of an unauthorised route, and a cargo ship went down after hitting a mine. A further tanker was burning in the Gulf of Oman, inside a zone where Tehran had warned that non-compliant vessels would face sanctions without ever saying what enforcement would involve. Brent traded at $104.42 on Saturday morning, down 1.6 per cent, and West Texas Intermediate at $94.11, down 1 per cent, which reads as a market pricing a toll on traffic rather than a closure of the strait. Those same prices were the reason Rubio gave for pausing Russia sanctions, so the Gulf and the Ukraine files are now being traded against each other inside one administration. Europe takes this through tanker insurance, LNG cargo economics and an oil price that is doing policy work in two capitals at once.

A riot weekend in France, and a protest wave that has crossed four borders

More than half a million people joined cost-of-living marches across France on Saturday and over 1,200 were arrested as the day ended in looting and arson, with a historic town hall in Limoges burning until its spire came down. The movement began last month in schools, over absent teachers and decrepit buildings, before widening to fuel prices and a fraying welfare system, and students, farmers and the Yellow Jacket movement marched together under a call for a convergence of struggles. Separate demonstrations the same weekend hit Belgium, Italy and Portugal, which makes this a European sequence rather than a French one, and autumn budgets are the common target. JD Vance posted footage of the burning city hall and attributed the violence to immigration, a reading none of the French reporting supports and one that converts a domestic fiscal argument into American campaign material. Paris has also opened an investigation into why 4x4 vehicles funded by the UK to stop Channel crossings were used to charge school students in Dunkirk, a small fact with an awkward reach into migration cooperation between London and Brussels.

Beijing drafts a 14-technology export ban while the subsidy arithmetic behind EU trade defence is contested

A draft Announcement No. 48 would ban exports of 14 technologies outright and restrict 26 more, next-generation solar processes among them, which bears on every country trying to build solar capacity away from Chinese supply. India is the clearest case, and Nirmala Sitharaman used the same week to tell Beijing that the bilateral deficit passed $100 billion in nine months and that market access for Indian goods has not moved, to which China replied that it is ready to address each other's concerns. The quantitative case Brussels and Washington lean on is meanwhile being taken apart in Beijing: the 2026 OECD MAGIC database puts state support for Chinese firms at three to eight times the OECD average between 2005 and 2024 and attributes almost 60 per cent of their global market-share gains to subsidies, with below-market borrowing doing most of the work in that gap. Li Yuankun of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences argues the gap is partly an artefact of the yardstick, since the Chinese component is measured against the Loan Prime Rate while OECD banks are compared with interbank funding, a cheaper reference and so a smaller implied subsidy; Zhu He and Guo Kai at CF40 and Cui Fan at the University of International Business and Economics have gone after the explanation and the estimation method separately. Anti-subsidy duties and economic-security screening both rest on numbers of this kind, so benchmark comparability is something DG TRADE wants settled before the next countervailing case rather than during it.

The chip lever Washington has not pulled, and the week that took 17.8 per cent off Samsung

Samsung Electronics fell 17.8 per cent across the week, from 262,000 won to 215,500, with SK hynix on the same track, the steepest five days for either since 2008 and driven by record foreign selling against worries about US rates, an AI capital-expenditure bubble and Chinese memory competition. The argument running in parallel in Washington is that deep-ultraviolet lithography tools are the lever nobody has pulled: the Center for Technology and Statecraft projects that halting tool exports now would leave China producing roughly a hundredth of the AI chips the United States does by the mid-2030s. Saif Khan, who worked on the existing export-control regime at the National Security Council and Commerce before leaving government, built that case on supply-chain mechanics rather than on forecasts of model capability, which is why it converts into regulation more readily than most AI policy. The capital-expenditure side is wobbling on its own terms, with the American data-centre build-out running into the economics of the physical world after a decade of treating it as solved. Three things that move European equipment and materials suppliers are therefore live at once: a possible DUV rule, a memory cycle turning, and a build-out whose financing assumptions are being reopened.

Longer-range missiles on Yonaguni and a satellite that reads the PLA in minutes

The Keen Sword exercise will have Japan field longer-range missiles while the United States moves advanced weapons onto Yonaguni, which places both capabilities on the island chain rather than in a rear area. General Ron Clark of US Army Pacific used the same week to argue that a war with China would not be settled at sea and in the air alone, because distance makes air and naval forces hard to rely on and land-based troops and equipment have to be in position beforehand. Taiwan is buying an AI satellite-imagery system for NT$46.55 million, about $1.45 million, that identifies PLA ships, aircraft and missile launchers across 100 square kilometres within minutes, turning early warning from a manpower problem into a software one. What is being assembled is pre-positioned land forces, dispersed missile fires and automated indications and warning, a combination that fields faster and cheaper than carrier groups. For Europe the consequence is a shorter warning time in any crisis and a procurement market in Tokyo and Taipei that now moves on software at least as much as on platforms.

Territory is back as an instrument, and the Falklands is the cheap version

Argentina's foreign minister, Pablo Quirno, has promised to obstruct Falklands oil development as a peaceful sovereignty campaign, and Javier Milei has told officials to pursue international arbitration against the Sea Lion project, run by the Israeli company Navitas Petroleum with the UK's Rockhopper Exploration and due to produce 50,000 barrels a day from 2028. The arithmetic explains the effort: one field would turn a fishing and tourism economy into a resource one, and interrupting it, or merely raising its cost, could thin the investment that makes British tenure worth paying for. Forty-four years after the war the dispute reopened over a World Cup celebration, and has since acquired a resource and an Argentine election cycle, which is how a dormant claim becomes a live one. The same pattern runs at larger scale through 2026, with Greenland, Taiwan and the frozen confrontations between India and Pakistan and on the Korean peninsula all resting on an assumption that industrial war between middle powers had been priced out of existence. For a European foreign service the consequence is that sovereignty disputes filed as legacy items need contingency work again, and the Falklands is the one where being wrong costs least.
