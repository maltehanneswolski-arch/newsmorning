Brussels lists 1,569 names while Washington buys Russian diesel

Foreign ministers signed off restrictive measures on 826 Russian companies and 743 individuals on Monday, the largest single set of listings since the invasion began, and added 77 more people over the State Duma elections Russia staged in September in the occupied territories of Ukraine. A separate tranche hit three individuals and four entities supplying North Korea's nuclear and missile programmes through Russian channels, folding the DPRK file into the Russia package rather than leaving it on its own track. The Foreign Affairs Council took the measures in the same sitting as the security consequences of enlargement, the hybrid campaign against EU territory, the Iran war and democratic backsliding in Georgia. All of it landed within two days of Washington agreeing to buy Russian diesel, so the two capitals are now pulling in opposite directions on the same target. European governments are calling for further measures as the United States eases off, which leaves the sanctions architecture carrying weight it was never designed to bear on its own.

The energy ceasefire nobody signed is already breaking

Russian strikes hit energy infrastructure in western Ukraine overnight and the state grid operator reported new power cuts in Kharkiv, Donetsk and Zaporizhzhia, hours after Trump announced a halt to attacks on energy targets. Zelensky told the Warsaw Security Forum on Monday that he learned of the ceasefire from the media, said it would be good news anyway, and then reported that Putin was already breaching it; the Kremlin has said nothing at all. Oil prices rose rather than fell on the announcement, which matters because the whole manoeuvre is aimed at US retail diesel that hit a record $6.50 a gallon in September. Washington is pressing Kyiv to stop striking Russian refineries so Moscow can deliver the fuel it promised, converting Ukraine's one effective deep-strike campaign into collateral in an American price problem. Trump said on Saturday it was time for Ukraine to get a new president, so Kyiv is choosing between defiance and the military support that defiance would cost it.

Berlin's coalition spends the last of its trust

Bärbel Bas resigned on Monday as labour minister and SPD co-chair after the death of her stepdaughter, with the party leadership saying the coalition partner would no longer grant her time to grieve. Alexander Schweitzer takes the labour ministry having just ended 35 years of SPD rule in Rhineland-Palatinate by losing it, and his first task is to organise the abolition of the deduction-free pension after 45 contribution years, which he defended as state premier. Anke Rehlinger has refused the party chairmanship and Manuela Schwesig the interim version, so Lars Klingbeil now wants the entire leadership re-elected in January. Merz speaks to the employers' federation today in the hall where Bas was laughed at a year ago, and he has to dispel the impression that the coalition is finished rather than merely bruised. He also joined Giorgia Meloni and Bart de Wever in asking Brussels for an emergency brake on new EU laws, so a chancellor short of authority at home is reaching for leverage abroad.

Parliament finds out whether its budget powers are real

Dublin's compromise on the 2028-34 budget, tabled on Saturday, takes 141 billion euro out of the Commission's spending plan, roughly 8 percent, while sparing farm and cohesion money. That arithmetic forces the reductions onto competitiveness, flexibility and the Commission's newer instruments, which is why Brussels says the proposal weakens the Union rather than trimming it. MEPs rejected the plan within hours as short-sighted and a risk of historic failure, and the early signs point to a Parliament spoiling for a fight rather than waving the package through. Co-decision on the budget took more than a decade of inter-institutional battles before Lisbon enshrined it, and nobody has yet tested whether the win is usable against a determined presidency. Leaders gather in Brussels this week with the economy formally on the agenda and this fight running underneath it.

Pyongyang tests while Seoul and Washington argue over what they tell each other

North Korea fired multiple ballistic missiles on Monday, its fourth test in a month, with the South Korean military putting the flight at 900 km and framing it as an answer to Seoul's recent hypersonic test. KCNA called the component upgrades verified in the launch a major achievement for the precision of all offensive weapons systems, and Kim Jong Un opened an arms museum displaying nuclear missiles and drones the same day. Underneath the launches the alliance is arguing with itself: Washington asked the National Intelligence Service to sit out a June working group on North Korean cyber threats, a senior US official said publicly that it expects Seoul to uphold alliance standards on intelligence and counterintelligence, and Seoul denies that most sharing has been curbed. The opposition People Power Party wants Unification Minister Chung Dong-young gone, tracing the rift to his March remarks about a nuclear facility at Kusong. Lee Jae Myung told a Cabinet meeting on Tuesday that the alliance matters but a submissive posture does not, a line easier to hold in a speech than in an intelligence channel.

Delhi's voter-roll revision draws the biggest march in twenty years

Somewhere between 50,000 and more than 200,000 people walked from Connaught Circus to the Supreme Court on Monday behind Congress and Left party cadres, one of the largest crowds central Delhi has seen in over two decades, demanding the election chief resign over the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls. Congress filed a petition seeking ten specific reliefs, among them reconstituting the selection committee for election commissioners to include the prime minister, the leader of the opposition in the Lok Sabha and the chief justice. The home ministry and Delhi police answered by denotifying Jantar Mantar as an authorised protest site and designating three grounds instead, two of them far enough from the centre to be invisible; youth leaders climbed the barricades there on Tuesday and were detained. The Committee to Protect Journalists counted at least 35 journalists detained in Delhi between 10 and 12 October, several of them assaulted, after the solicitor-general had assured the Supreme Court that journalists would not be impeded. Dozens of trains into the capital were cancelled, which is the plainest measure of how the government reads the size of the problem.

Europe buys time on Chinese cars and argues about how to spend it

Brussels and Beijing reached an understanding that shields the hybrid segment from Chinese imports, which European carmakers welcomed as a first step towards a fairer field while other industries warned that China's trade surplus will not move without more. The respite is read as temporary even by the people who asked for it, and Macron says a Franco-German deal on car policy is imminent that would push back the combustion-engine phaseout and lift production on the continent. What the truce defers is the structural choice: extract real concessions on the imbalance, or reach for unilateral instruments to protect the industrial base. On the demand side the Industrial Accelerator Act is meant to tie public spending to European and low-carbon production, but as drafted it would cover only a small share of purchases in most sectors and its exemptions let governments opt out too easily. Philipp Jäger and Nils Redeker put the workable landing zone at two moves: keep the regime open to trade partners where fair competition and supply security allow while hardening anti-circumvention rules, and give local-content and low-carbon requirements enough bite to pull manufacturing investment.

Seoul builds a chip office as the AI trade wobbles under it

A director-general-level semiconductor policy office opens next week under the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources to fast-track roughly 800 trillion won of investment and implement a new industry-support law, following Cabinet approval of the reorganisation. The IMF lifted South Korea's 2026 growth forecast to 3.3 percent, 0.7 points above its July call and above the 3.1 percent it expects for the world, crediting semiconductor exports with more than offsetting the Middle East war. On the same day Samsung and SK Hynix fell hard enough to drag the Kospi down after Broadcom's chief executive described parts of the AI market as a casino, so the cycle underwriting that forecast is being repriced while the policy is still being drafted. Samsung used the Open Compute Project summit in San Jose to set out eighth-generation HBM5, custom high-bandwidth memory and processing-in-memory for AI data centres, which is where the export revenue actually comes from. An external account riding on one industry is a concentrated bet at a moment when the IMF is warning in the same report that AI investment is propping growth up while war and debt pull the other way.

Three incompatible readings of Russian strength, and policy due anyway

NATO's supreme allied commander Alexus Grynkewich warned Moscow that the alliance would answer an attack immediately, while European officials catalogue a run of covert activity that includes an arson attack on an Estonian defence company and a failed attempt to strike a Ukrainian aircraft at a German airport. His own framing is that the hybrid campaign is unsettling even though a conventional conflict is not imminent, a distinction European planners keep having to explain to their own publics. The Foreign Affairs Council put the escalating hybrid campaign on its agenda the same afternoon it adopted the sanctions package, so the two instruments are at least being sequenced together. Putin used the Valdai Club to argue the reverse case, that European rearmament and rhetoric are manufacturing the escalation they claim to prevent, and cast Moscow's Kaliningrad warnings as a reply to attempts at intimidation. Ukraine's former commander-in-chief Syrskyi told the BBC in his first interview since being removed that Russia's offensive capability is broken, which leaves capitals setting defence budgets against three readings of Russian strength that cannot all be right.

The Gulf war is setting the price of everything else

Saudi air defences failed against a Houthi barrage that killed at least 13 people and wounded more than 300 at Riyadh airport, and an initial probe indicates drones were sent in first to clear a path for cruise missiles. A second strike took the toll to 14, the Houthis claimed hits on Saudi energy infrastructure, a ship carrying Saudi crude struck a mine in the Strait of Hormuz, and Trump said the United States may join Saudi retaliation. The IMF's new outlook has AI investment holding global growth at 3.1 percent while war and debt pull against it, and flags a widening gap between economies capturing AI demand and those bypassed by it. Southeast Asian economies are still expanding through the war, but the inflation it generates is landing on the poorest households, and Pakistan faces a direct choice about how far to back Riyadh. For EU foreign policy the causal chain runs backwards from the usual direction: Gulf escalation sets European fuel costs, which set the politics of the Russian-diesel argument in Washington.

Runs the Union's delegations worldwide, prepares the Foreign Affairs Council and drafts the sanctions and strategy papers member states then argue over.
