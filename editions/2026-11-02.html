Background on today's names

Person

Jarosław Kaczyński

Poland · b. 1949

Chairman of Law and Justice 2003- · Prime Minister of Poland 2006-07 · Member of the Sejm

Co-founder of Law and Justice with his twin brother Lech and its chairman for more than two decades, he governed for most of the party's 2015-23 period in office from the party chair rather than from a ministry, which is the arrangement Polish commentary meant by calling him the decisive figure without portfolio.

Why it matters. He led supporters against the Constitutional Tribunal building in Warsaw on 30 October and told a Law and Justice rally in Krynica that Poland should threaten to leave the EU unless the European Commission is dismantled, which puts a Polexit demand inside the court fight for the first time.

He and his twin brother Lech, later president, starred as child actors in the 1962 Polish film O dwoch takich, co ukradli ksiezyc.

Institution

CJEU

Court of Justice of the European Union · Luxembourg

Judicial authority of the EU · one judge per member state on the Court of Justice, with eleven advocates general · sits alongside the General Court

Created in 1952 for the Coal and Steel Community and now interpreting EU law for all twenty-seven members. Most of its docket arrives as preliminary references under Article 267 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU, in which a national judge stops a case to ask how a European rule is to be read.

Why it matters. Its December ruling that the previous Polish government's appointments to the Constitutional Tribunal were unlawful is the legal ground Tusk cites for replacing the tribunal's leadership, and the reason the Commission has taken his side rather than the president's.

Its 1964 Costa v ENEL judgment established that European law takes precedence over conflicting national law, a rule found nowhere in the treaties themselves.

Warsaw fights over who controls the constitutional court

Bogdan Swieczkowski was relieved of the presidency of Poland's Constitutional Tribunal on Friday, called the move a coup, and was replaced on an acting basis by Anna Korwin-Piotrowska; President Karol Nawrocki then got himself into the sealed court building overnight into Saturday and tussled with her on the way in [Handelsblatt]. The underlying blockage is narrower than the scenes suggest: Nawrocki has declined to swear in four of the six judges a Sejm majority elected, alleging flaws in their selection, and without that oath the tribunal cannot seat them [The Hindu]. Jaroslaw Kaczynski widened it on 30 October by leading supporters against the same building for the cameras, then told a Law and Justice rally in Krynica that Poland should threaten to leave the EU unless it agrees to dismantle the European Commission, which he called the root of all evil — the opening move of a 2027 comeback bid alongside the Confederation of Liberty and Independence and the Confederation of the Polish Crown [EUobserver]. Donald Tusk says he will not retreat and accuses the opposition of stirring violence to break the rule of law, with Marine Le Pen and Janez Jansa already lining up on the other side [The Japan Times, EURACTIV]. For Brussels the file is no longer about restoring a court but about a member state where both the restoration and the resistance to it are being staged on the street, five months before an election polling close.

Merz meets his district chairmen with the party at a low

The CDU calls its first conference of district chairmen in five years at 14:00 today in the Konrad-Adenauer-Haus, behind closed doors, with the chancellor expected to explain how he stops the party's slide. There are 327 district associations and roughly half their chairmen hold a federal, state or European mandate, so the room is not simply a grassroots delegation. One demand circulating is to split the party chair from the chancellorship again, which Serap Güler, Cologne district chair and minister of state at the Federal Foreign Office, rejects while conceding there is anger at the base [POLITICO Berlin Playbook]. Three other files land on Merz this week: the succession to Steinmeier, a pension package he has called the largest reform since Adenauer and whose 2027 uprating is about to become public, and a budget where the electricity tax cut he wants for all consumers costs five billion euros and still needs Lars Klingbeil's agreement. All of it is being decided against a third weekend of cost-of-living marches, and this one did not stay peaceful [BBC News - Europe, Financial Times - Europe].

An American midterm that prices in everyone else

Americans elect Congress on Tuesday, and the president has repeatedly asked supporters to treat the ballot as though his own name were on it, which hands his critics the frame for any Republican loss. A sweeping Democratic win would end the legislative agenda that produced last year's tax cuts and immigration enforcement money, and would open committee investigations running from the president's financial interests to the dismantling of federal agencies. The first foreign-policy consequence would arrive faster than any of that: POLITICO's Jonathan Martin reads a catastrophic defeat as putting the defence secretary and the Iran war in immediate question, on the 2006 precedent of Bush sacking Rumsfeld the morning after [POLITICO Forecast]. European diplomats expect the opposite of restraint, and some fear a president constrained at home will move thousands more troops out of Europe or cut NATO funding to fix a legacy while he still can [POLITICO Europe]. Chinese analysts reach the same conclusion from the other side, doubting a lame-duck phase would deter more transactional or risky moves abroad [South China Morning Post]; meanwhile petrol prices are set to touch a record high on the eve of a vote where the cost of living is the chief concern [Financial Times].

Seoul's market is one industry wearing a country's name

The KOSPI rose 241 percent between March 2025 and June 2026, adding close to 3.3 trillion dollars and exceeding everything the index had accumulated in its previous 43 years, on commitments that included 880 billion dollars of fabs and data centres announced by SK, Samsung and Naver in June — more than half of annualised Korean GDP. It then fell more than a quarter between 22 June and 16 July, erasing roughly 1.1 trillion dollars, with SK hynix down 38 percent and Samsung 29 percent; Micron lost about a third in the same window, which is the clue that the correction belonged to an industry rather than to a country. By July, Samsung Electronics and SK hynix alone were more than half of total KOSPI capitalisation, against 37.2 percent for the Magnificent Seven inside the S&P 500, and equity outflows exceeded 35 billion dollars across May and June. The Financial Services Commission answered by suspending sales of single-stock leveraged exchange-traded funds and tripling the minimum deposit to trade them to 20,300 dollars from 5 August. The pair held 83 percent of high-bandwidth memory and 63 percent of dynamic random-access memory in the second quarter, so the arrival of a state-backed Chinese entrant, CXMT, prices through to every balance sheet in the AI stack rather than to Korea alone [ChinaTalk].

The 2028 data-centre pipeline is on this week's ballot

About three-quarters of the 33.8 gigawatts due across the top twenty American states next year is already under construction, which puts 2027 largely beyond the reach of Tuesday's result. The exposure is 2028: 62.2 gigawatts of net additions are modelled nationally, most of it not yet begun, and 43.5 gigawatts of that — seventy percent of the national figure — sits in thirteen states, ten of which elect a governor on 3 November. Restrictions have widened since the summer: Texas froze Texas Commission on Environmental Quality permits on 21 September, on top of an interconnection-queue audit started on 3 August, which brings onsite power generation inside the hold; Oregon extended its state-land freeze to air and water-discharge permits on 9 October, running to 1 July 2027. Nine of the twenty gubernatorial nominees in those states want a pause on new construction and the remaining eleven want conditions attached, a split that runs roughly evenly across both parties, so the live question is whether a developer can meet new terms and keep building or has to wait for a state to resume issuing approvals at all. The Electric Reliability Council of Texas is targeting 10 December for its audit report, which does not by itself restart permitting; a freeze held through the legislative session that runs 12 January to 31 May 2027 would reach about eight months, and a campus aiming at 2027 delivery does not have that much slack [SemiAnalysis].

Washington, Seoul and Tokyo sign a framework with nothing in it yet

Marco Rubio, Cho Hyun and Toshimitsu Motegi signed a memorandum in Atlanta on Friday creating a trilateral Arctic Pacific Partnership, with a working group to explore maritime safety, emergency response and possible economic activity in the Pacific Arctic. The document names no projects, no funding and no implementation deadlines, which makes it a placeholder for a theatre the three governments expect to matter rather than a programme. Rubio used the same meeting to raise Chinese maritime activity and Russia-China cooperation, while Seoul restated its support for dialogue with Pyongyang — the familiar divergence inside this format, and the reason its communiqués stay general. North Korea replied on Monday through Kim Jong-sik of the Workers' Party Central Committee, condemning the recent exercise that paired American nuclear capabilities with South Korean conventional forces as a rehearsal for nuclear war and calling an accelerated buildup an immutable policy [The Korea Herald]. The parts with European consequences are the two the ministers reviewed in passing: small modular reactors and supply-chain security, where the same Korean vendors are bidding into European nuclear and battery programmes.

Beijing turns the discipline machine on its own technology sector

The fifth plenum of the 20th Central Committee confirmed the expulsion of 33 of its own members, sixteen of them generals, which leaves open questions about People's Liberation Army command and readiness that the communiqué does not address [The Hindu]. The document adopted on Thursday extends party building across the technology sector and commits to a digital discipline inspection platform and a big-data supervision platform, alongside a crackdown on corruption involving the internet, virtual currency, cross-border funds and blockchain. Unwritten rules — the informal understandings that make patronage work without instructions — are named as the campaign's newest target, which is a shift from prosecuting transactions to prosecuting conventions. Xi Jinping took the floor as the plenum closed to defend strict internal governance as a fundamental guarantee of China's rise and to warn against any easing of scrutiny, an answer to the argument that the campaign is costing the economy growth. None of it clarifies succession ahead of the 2027 Party Congress, which is the question the readout was read for.

The longest truce of the war ends, and the alert apps go first

The six-day ceasefire around Oleshky in occupied Kherson, the most substantial pause since the full-scale invasion began almost five years ago, expired on Sunday evening after Moscow refused Kyiv's offer to extend it; the White House says the first lady helped broker it, and it held long enough for a humanitarian mission into the city [Le Monde, The Korea Herald]. Within hours Ukrainian users of the national air-raid alert system were receiving fabricated nuclear warnings, and Moscow reported false information posted through its own emergency app, each side attributing the other's breach to the other [Al Jazeera, The Japan Times]. Kyiv has signalled it would stop striking Russian oil refineries in exchange for Patriot interceptors, which converts a campaign it can sustain into a bargaining chip it can trade [France 24]. Russian strikes meanwhile left thousands without power, and Moscow reported significant damage to an industrial facility in Nizhny Novgorod [POLITICO Europe]. Targeting civil-defence messaging rather than the grid is the part worth noting: it degrades the warning layer that makes the grid survivable, and it is cheap enough to repeat.

Coastguard hulls, not warships, do the work around Taiwan

China has extended its maritime presence from Taiwan's eastern approaches to waters near Quemoy and the remote Dongsha atoll, with the coastguard rather than the navy carrying the activity — a formation led by the cutter Hushi has been working the eastern side since October and is described in Beijing's own language as reinforcing administrative control [South China Morning Post]. The framing matters more than the hulls: law-enforcement patrols assert jurisdiction without producing the military incident that would trigger allied consultation, which is the same instrument used around the Senkakus and in the South China Sea. Taipei has chosen visibility as its answer, publicising more than fifty warships and official vessels strung along the island arc from southern Japan to the Philippines and shadowing the ships rather than intercepting them [bne IntelliNews]. Shenzhen is two weeks from hosting the APEC leaders' summit, with crews laying pavements and planting trees through the night, so Beijing has a fortnight in which a visible incident would be expensive to explain. That is the window in which pressure is most likely to stay administrative, and the one after it is the window to watch.

The quiet phase of the trade war is where the dependencies deepen

China's total exports rose 5.5 percent in 2025 while its shipments to the United States fell about twenty percent, and much of the displaced volume landed in the European Union — tariff walls redirect trade before they reduce it [CSIS]. Chemicals are the candidate for the next front, with output of some key ingredients almost doubling in five years and foreign producers now facing the price consequences [Financial Times]. The dependency argument has moved past rare earths: solar, drone and drug-making technology are all harder to source elsewhere than they were, which narrows the menu of credible European substitution [New York Times]. On the other side of the ledger, billions in American backing for critical minerals have produced a rush of deals and lobbying rather than measurable domestic production [Financial Times]. Beijing is applying the same upgrade logic to the African arms market, moving from small arms and artillery into air defence, armed drones and electronic warfare as Russian and Western suppliers pull back [South China Morning Post]. The truce has cooled the conflict without doing anything to limit a fresh outbreak, which is the reading EU trade policy should plan against [The Guardian].
