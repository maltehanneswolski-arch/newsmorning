Know the players

Background on today's names

Person 🇪🇺

Kaja Kallas

b. 1977 · Estonia

High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy · Vice-President of the European Commission

Estonian prime minister 2021-2024, lawyer by training, daughter of a former European commissioner and Bank of Estonia governor. Took the Union's foreign policy post in December 2024 after building a reputation as the loudest northern voice on Russia.

Why it matters. She is carrying four separate files in one news cycle: the charge that Washington is pushing Ukraine toward surrender, support for a Black Sea naval mission, the alarm over cuts to external aid, and a warning about a perfect storm in food prices. Each is a case where the Union's instrument is money or words rather than force, which is the constraint the job actually operates under.

Her first act on taking office was a trip to Kyiv, and she has argued publicly that European support is the only reason Ukraine can still resist.

Institution 🇪🇺

European Parliament

Strasbourg and Brussels · 720 members

Co-legislator on the EU budget · consent power over international agreements

Shares budgetary authority with the Council and must consent to trade and association agreements, which is where its foreign policy weight actually sits. It has no say over the common foreign and security policy itself, and compensates by using the money and the consent votes.

Why it matters. It has refused the governments' cut to external aid in the next seven-year budget and demanded 25 billion euros more for foreign policy, while its largest group wants Black Sea security funded from the same envelope and is reopening two climate files. Every one of those positions is leverage that only exists because the budget needs its signature.

Its consent requirement has killed finished agreements before, most memorably the ACTA counterfeiting treaty in 2012, which is why trade negotiators brief it long before a text is final.

Witkoff lands in St Petersburg with the intelligence tap as the bargaining chip

Steve Witkoff arrived in St Petersburg on Wednesday to put proposals for ending the war directly to Vladimir Putin, a day after he and Jared Kushner met Volodymyr Zelensky and European leaders in Oslo. Zelensky now says American intelligence-sharing works again, and that is the sentence to read twice: Kyiv had been warning that it was no longer getting reliable alerts on incoming attacks and that people were dying for the gap, and the flow came back as the talks opened. Finland's president called the Oslo round progress, Ukrainian officials complained in the same days of a hold-up on missile deliveries, and Marco Rubio has been repeating Moscow's inflated account of Russian gains in Donetsk while Ukrainian crews fly heavy drones into territory Russia says it holds to recover their own wounded. One resource Washington can switch off, a second it can slow down, and a public line that matches the Kremlin's: that is leverage pointed at Kyiv rather than at Moscow. European officials have started saying out loud that the problem may be personal rather than strategic, which is a worse diagnosis, because a preference cannot be argued with the way an interest can.

Brussels hunts 70 billion euros for Kyiv while trimming the aid budget that buys it influence

Keeping Ukraine funded through next year is priced at about 70 billion euros, and with Washington's contribution uncertain Brussels is canvassing Canada, Britain and Japan, conversations that run straight into the EU-Canada summit on 29 and 30 October and the EU-UK summit in Brussels on 20 November. The same institutions are fighting over the money they already have: governments have taken an axe to external aid in the next seven-year budget, and members of the European Parliament have refused the terms outright, demanding an extra 25 billion euros for foreign policy instead. Kaja Kallas has sounded the alarm on the aid line in particular, which is the part of the budget that buys access rather than hardware. The EPP wants Black Sea security financed from the same envelope and is taking members to the Bulgarian coast after the suspected Russian drone attacks there, so the Parliament is asking for more money and for more things to spend it on at once. Whoever loses this argument sets what the Union can actually do abroad until 2034, which makes the multiannual financial framework the live foreign-policy file this autumn rather than any strategy paper.

Grain ships are taking drone hits and no European navy is coming

Kallas has backed the idea of a Black Sea naval mission to shield grain shipping from Russian drones, and the reading inside the institutions is that it will not happen: it would demand far more than the bloc's existing Red Sea operation, and air policing would deliver more per euro spent. Phil Hogan, the former Irish commissioner now bidding to lead the UN's food agency, wants that agency drawn into talks with Moscow and Kyiv on securing the route, which concedes that the fix on offer is diplomatic rather than naval. Kallas has separately described a perfect storm building on food prices, with fertiliser costs climbing and trade disrupted, which is the same corridor read from the supermarket shelf. Suspected drone attacks on shipping are what is pulling members of the European Parliament toward Bulgaria's coast and toward funding Black Sea security in the next budget. Until one of those tracks produces something physical, the corridor is defended by argument, and the argument is slower than the drones are.

France burns six months before it votes, and both fringes stand to collect

Sebastien Lecornu promised a massive police deployment for protests called this coming weekend after admitting to angry lawmakers that the security response to Saturday's rioting had fallen short, the worst street violence France has seen in years and six months before the first round of the presidential election in April. The chain is legible: school protests, the left joining in, then casseurs setting fires and looting, a town hall roof collapsing in one blaze, and a suspect linked to a far-left movement arrested over it. Marine Le Pen leads the polls and Jean-Luc Melenchon has aligned himself with the student protesters, so both ends of the spectrum are placed to profit while the centre owns the disorder. Brussels has opened an inquiry into pro-Kremlin disinformation circulating around the riots and Italy is sending 500 troops to Milan after unrest of its own, which makes this a European file rather than a French one.

The AI build-out is being financed off balance sheet and the bill lands in 2027

Microsoft, Meta, Alphabet, Amazon and Oracle are on course for roughly 800 billion dollars of AI investment this year and about 1.1 trillion in 2027, and the Flossbach von Storch Research Institute puts their combined leases and other commitments for infrastructure and energy at 2.7 trillion dollars, obligations that mostly surface on a balance sheet only once the data centres are finished. The same five companies have gone from a 209 billion dollar net cash cushion in June 2021 to 106 billion in adjusted net debt, and trading in credit default swaps on their bonds has multiplied. Korea felt the second-order version first, with the Kospi falling 7.57 per cent in a single session and triggering a 20-minute market-wide halt around Samsung Electronics and SK hynix, before Nomura and JPMorgan restated the case for memory and the index swung back. Rising rates make every future financing round dearer, which is the mechanism by which a wobble in AI demand becomes an industrial problem for chip and power suppliers two continents away. European exposure runs through the same chain of equipment orders, grid connections and the energy contracts underwriting all of it.

Washington bars DeepSeek from federal systems as Beijing drafts its own export rules

The Department of Homeland Security has ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek products and, more consequentially, any third-party product or service built on DeepSeek, which is a supply-chain rule rather than a procurement ban because the dependency is usually invisible to the buyer. Beijing called the order discriminatory and is at the same time drafting export rules for its own frontier models, trying to hold a national-security grip on the technology without giving up the diffusion advantage that open weights deliver. That diffusion is already measurable next door: close to half of the AI models released by South Korean developers this year were built on Chinese foundations, Alibaba's Qwen above all, according to a study by the think tank Rockbridge Network Korea. Japan has its own version, with the internet group GMO attributing a run of cyberattacks to a Chinese-made AI tool. Xi and Trump have agreed a formal AI dialogue and a hotline with the next meeting due in November, so a channel exists, and Europe, holding neither the frontier models nor the export controls, will inherit whatever the two of them settle on.

🇰🇷 South Korea

Exit bans enter the toolkit for unpaid health insurance

The government picked 30 tasks from roughly 200 ministry proposals in the second round of its national normalisation project, among them legal grounds to request exit bans on foreign nationals who fail to pay large health insurance premiums without good reason. The notification threshold to the Justice Ministry drops from 500,000 to 400,000 won, reciprocity gets applied to pension back-payments, and foreign companies posing as liaison offices to avoid tax are targeted. Several items need the National Assembly, but anything doable by decree is meant to land this year and will be scored in ministry evaluations. European firms with staff in Korea should read the liaison-office line closely.
