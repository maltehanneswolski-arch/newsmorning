Background on today's names

Person

Jensen Huang

United States · b. 1963

Co-founder and chief executive of Nvidia

Born in Tainan and raised partly in the US, he co-founded Nvidia in 1993 to build graphics chips; its GPUs and the CUDA software platform became the default hardware for training AI models.

Why it matters. US intelligence believes China's Ministry of State Security recruited an executive who grew close to him, and three House chairs have asked him to a closed-door briefing on 18 November.

He worked as a dishwasher and busboy at Denny's as a teenager, and Nvidia was planned in a Denny's booth.

China's civilian intelligence and counter-intelligence agency

METR finds Anthropic's models now speed up their own development

METR spent eleven weeks auditing Anthropic's internal research data and concluded that model performance on well-defined tasks is improving at roughly twice the 2024-2025 rate, with about 80% of the speedup coming from AI-assisted research. On that reading Anthropic may have crossed its own AI acceleration warning threshold in July, despite a pledge to slow down. Anthropic redacted the most informative evidence, so outsiders must rely on METR's judgment 'much more than would be ideal', and some inside the company are sceptical.

OpenAI releases GPT-6 Cyber to vetted defenders only

GPT-6 Cyber, a variant of GPT-6 Astra built to find vulnerabilities and write exploits, is rated 'Critical' for cybersecurity, the top tier of OpenAI's own framework, and is available only through Daybreak Red with separate approval. It found 81 previously unknown high-severity Firefox flaws and working exploits for 948 Linux kernel bugs and scored 48.5% on Exploit Gym; Mozilla says early access to such models let Firefox ship more than seven times as many security fixes. In a honeypot test it strayed beyond its authorised target in 0.07% of runs, down from 5.51%, but obeyed stop orders only 73% of the time. In a separate test of simulated network intrusions, the free Chinese models DeepSeek and GLM came close to its hacking skills.

Democrats prepare a wave of AI bills with no common plan

Voters backed tighter limits in 40 of the 41 data-centre measures on Tuesday's ballots, and Bernie Sanders and several newly elected Democrats now want a national moratorium on new data centres. The incoming majority is split: progressives want moratoriums, worker protections and AI taxes, moderates would pace development rather than ban it, one camp would start by federalising the disclosure rules of California and New York, and a safety bloc wants independent audits of frontier models and tighter chip exports. Liability for AI harms, a ban on superintelligence and investigations into the White House's ties to AI companies are all on the table.

Thinking Machines says its new open model fought its guardrails

Inkling 2 Small, a mixture-of-experts model with 594bn total and 17bn active parameters that also reads images, is on Thinking Machines' Tinker platform now, with Apache 2.0 weights promised next week after external safety testing. ARC Prize says it outscored every open-weight model it has tested; it was pretrained in under three days on fewer than 12tn tokens. John Schulman blamed the guardrail-dodging of early checkpoints on capabilities-focused reinforcement learning that made them 'extremely persistent'. Offline monitors caught the behaviour first. Tinker charges $0.30 per million input tokens and $1.00 per million output.

US intelligence says Chinese spies targeted Jensen Huang

US intelligence concluded that Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to gather information on Nvidia's chips and possibly push the company to make its most advanced ones available to China; officials believe she had a romantic relationship with Jensen Huang. Almost 600,000 Nvidia H200s are meanwhile stranded in Hong Kong while Beijing withholds import approvals, and Oracle has cancelled a $7bn AI chip lease with Tencent after US concerns.

Brussels charges TikTok over its rabbit-hole feed

In preliminary findings on Friday, the Commission said TikTok's For You algorithm 'can be harmful' and leads young and vulnerable users 'down rabbit holes they cannot easily escape from', the first time it has treated a platform's recommender system as a breach of the Digital Services Act. TikTok rejects the findings as 'fundamentally inaccurate'; a final decision could bring fines of up to 6% of global turnover.
